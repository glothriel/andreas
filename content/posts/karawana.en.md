It still hasn't sunk in for Jan – he is at the airport in Athens. The year nineteen eighty-five. A visa for a one-month stay, obtained with difficulty, and to get it you needed an invitation, sent by Sakis. Sakis is a Greek born in Poland, the son of political emigrants, who a few years earlier left and settled in the country of his parents.

Jan crumples his ten dollars in his fist. That's how much he was allowed to change officially. And in the arrivals hall, shops like swanky Pewex stores. Liquor from all over the world, unknown brands of cigarettes, vinyl records on the ECM label, Jan Garbarek, Pink Floyd, Procol Harum, John Abercrombie, jeans… He strolls about and waits. An hour passes, then two. Something must have come up for Sakis. He smooths out the ten-dollar bill and waits. Despite the air conditioning, heavy sweat trickles down onto his shorts and reaches his woven sandals.

After twelve hours he changes the dollars into drachmas and gets into a taxi. He shows the driver a slip of paper with the address of a girl he knows, who came here just before martial law and married a Greek. He pays the fare, realising that he has spent every drachma on it. He finds a small house squeezed in between two tall buildings. The number matches, the name too. He rings. Dark windows and silence.

A sweltering August night, stifling, soaked to the marrow with the damp of the breeze from the Aegean Sea, which together with the ominous grey cloud of exhaust fumes that covers the basin of the Attic valley like a canopy. All this makes the people who have dared to go out into the streets to get to the shops, tavernas and cafés, these people drag their feet, panting heavily, and slither along like fish pulled out of water. Drenched in sweat, Jan remembers that August is the traditional holiday month in Greece. Everything has fallen apart. What to do? He definitely has to go back to the airport. Sakis assured him that cash was no problem. Okey.

He sits at a bus stop and watches the wide road along which swanky rides go speeding by. Hondas, Suzukis, Volvos, Fords, Jeeps, and that's not the same as Polonezes or little Fiats. But what do I care – he thinks – I've got to get to that damned airport. This is not how he imagined the world behind the Iron Curtain. Luxury mixes here with squalid poverty, rubbish lies about everywhere, there are no pavements, illegal building squeezes in, steals scraps of the road and blocks what's left of the pavements with concrete billboard frames.

Though greenery is as scarce as medicine, opposite Jan a spreading plane tree reigns, under which men sit at tables, drink beer and argue noisily. The leftists or Olympiacos fans drink Amstel, because it has a red label, and those who support Panathinaikos drink Heineken, because it has a green label, just like that team's club colours. Then a waiter grinning from ear to ear comes up to them and hands a rubber hose to the first one at hand, and the guy kicks off his plastic Chinese flip-flops and cools his sweaty feet with a stream of cold water. Now the men, regardless of club allegiance and political preference, quarrel over access to the hose, and more than one of them gets an unwanted shower.

But Jan is in no mood for laughing. He sits there glum, chewing in his clenched mouth choice words from the repertoire of Polish Latin, and the whores fly about, merrily piercing with their brooms the ancient sky under which Socrates pissed off the citizens of Athens, and they sentenced him to death for it. One of the beer drinkers notices Jan and invites him to join them, and he makes his way under the plane tree and, partly in sign language, partly in broken English, lays out his situation. The Greeks, shouting over one another, often repeating the word malakas, hand Jan a wad of banknotes and push him, pointing at the same time at the wall clock, to the other side of the road. And at the last moment he gets on a bus about to leave and, relieved, waves goodbye to them. The smile on his face widens even further, almost touching, left and right, his ears, when at the airport he meets Sakis. The friends fall into each other's arms, and then immediately get on the next bus.

Dawn breaks in the port of Piraeus. The ship they will travel on is already taking in lorries, cars and two-wheelers. On the quay horns blare, the siren of a huge cruise liner leaving port howls. A guy with a cart fixed to his bicycle, shouting over the noise, cries up his sesame-sprinkled bread rings. A grey, portly bearded man yells by a glazed portable stand that he is offering, fresh from the bakery, fragrant pies with feta, with spinach, and sweet ones with custard or with apples. Gypsies press ballpoint pens and tissues on the travellers, and on top of that beggar women with infants wrapped in rags wave pieces of cardboard with pleading words in English, and crocodile tears run down their faces. Jan and Sakis sit at a table on the upper deck of a floating vessel by the name of Demeter. Jan tells him that he has landed an engagement at a provincial but ambitious theatre and will play one of the leading roles. Sakis says that he is only just looking for his place in his parents' homeland and for now he's busy painting and selling little pictures done in acrylics on small boards, with motifs from the islands of the Cyclades archipelago.

On their table, next to a wheat roll, stand a tin of Polish luncheon meat and a container of Greek feta in brine, and under the table a bottle of clear rye vodka sticks its neck out of a backpack. German girls, blondes from Scandinavia and juicy Italian women bare their charms to the Aegean sun, and old Greek women, all in black, look at them scandalised. That's their custom, that once they become widows they have to go about outside the house in outfits of black colour. Jan's eyes shine with the orange, goggling ball of the full moon when the wolf howls, calling up the pack, and in his boxers there is quite a commotion. But he keeps a cold face, washing down his arousal with repulsively warm vodka.

– Get ready, old man – says Sakis – we're getting off at Naxos.

– What do you mean? We were supposed to sail to Santorini.

– Don't get worked up, we have to earn the rest of the trip on the spot.

– I hope you know what you're doing. Although…

In the crowd of tourists who got off in the port of Naxos, Jan trudges along, laden with a heavy backpack with a sleeping bag strapped on top, which with every step presses annoyingly against his skull, shaved to the skin, and ahead of him, with a light step, strides Sakis, a travel bag slung over his shoulder. The crowd slowly thins out, some get into minibuses, others into hired cars, and still others, led by noisy guides, scatter through the winding, narrow alleys climbing from the port to the little town on the hill.

Out of this crowd a procession of a dozen or so people separates itself. Young couples in dreadlocks, long-haired guys of various ages, girls skimpily dressed in tight denim gear and a few middle-aged couples who, you can see it clearly, don't feel at ease among the others, and speak to each other in some Eastern European language. In this peculiar caravan Sakis bustles about, talking with the walkers a bit in English, a bit in Greek, and even in Russian and in Polish. They reach the bus depot and almost all of them get into one of the vehicles. Only two couples from Hungary give in to the persuasion of a local wise guy, who is to take them to rooms for rent.

An old hunchbacked Volvo with a roof rack laboriously rattles along a bumpy dirt road, moving away from the port. On the roadsides dusty, spreading fig trees with ripe fruit split by the sun bow to vehicles and passers-by. Small plots are cut through with hedges of prickly pear. These cacti, with their thick, fat, plate-like leaves fortified with thousands of spines, serve as protection against the wind, and also against the designs of the goats, sheep and cows grazing here and there. And in the vineyards the villagers, making use of the brisk evening breeze, cut ripe grapes and load them into wicker baskets, and with these baskets they burden the little donkeys, braying piteously and protesting. Jan soaks it all in, enchanted, and for the here and now forgets his worries.

The bus stops in the central little square of a small village. In the middle of the square, under an enormous plane tree, from a copper pipe sticking out of the rocks, water from a spring in the nearby mountains flows in an unbroken stream into a marble trough. Dominating the buildings is an Orthodox church whitewashed with lime, with a blue dome and a three-part bell tower. Next to it stands a wooden kiosk with a rectangular umbrella attached, under which postcards, newspapers and magazines pile up on stands. Two upright fridges tempt with ice-cold beer in cans, drinks in bottles and juices in cartons, and behind the glass of a long fridge multicoloured ice creams lure with exquisite flavours. Apart from that, tacky Chinese souvenirs, which have nothing whatsoever to do with this island, glare from a few shelves.

Through the open windows of the bus, which is already setting off further on its way, smoke forces its way in, full of the intoxicating smells of fish grilled on the barbecue, octopus, squid, kebabs, lamb ribs, narrow long peppers, courgettes and aubergines sliced lengthwise. Jan furtively wipes away the drool running from the corner of his mouth and with undisguised reproach watches Sakis, who, laid-back, is chatting with a pimply youngster in dreadlocks.

The dirt road ends at a makeshift car park separated from the beach by a row of tamarisks. The whole little band, having crossed the border of these stunted trees dripping with a mixture of grains of sand and condensed sea spray, this whole international company spills out onto a golden beach which, as far as the eye can see, stretches all the way to the horizon. Almost everyone pulls off their shoes or sandals and frolics along the shore, cooling their bare feet in the brisk, gentle waves. Only Jan, laden with his overheavy luggage, shuffles along, wading and sinking into the loose sand.

A magical evening falls and the sun, at a slow pace, plunges into the depths of the Aegean Sea. A dry, choking Libyan wind dusts everything around with red powder born of the sands of the Sahara. And then the same wind rocks the maidens of Keros lying on the oily surface of the sea, who, with arms folded, are praying, bidding farewell to the scorching day, and it tugs at the mast of the boat where Odysseus, tied up, with his senses of sight and hearing switched off, defends himself against the deadly advances of the Sirens. The row of tamarisks turns sharply inland and Jan notices with relief that, together with the whole motley band, he has found himself in a small cove drowning in the light of electric lamps. A little above the shore, in the centre of this cove, reigns a taverna surrounded by shelters covered with straw mats, under which tourists sit at tables. There's a smell of aniseed and fried fish. And, what particularly catches Jan's attention, scattered across the beach are a dozen or so huts made of reed. The newcomers throw down their luggage by these huts and run towards the taverna.

– Make yourself at home – says Sakis – we'll meet in Paradise.

– In Paradise?

– In the Paradise taverna. We're invited.

It is the third day of the two friends' stay on the island of Naxos. Evening. Sakis comes back from the promenade and announces that again he hasn't earned anything and that with his last drachmas he bought a packet of peanuts, but there's going to be a party by a bonfire on the beach and they've got grub and booze guaranteed and maybe something more. Jan is worried, but doesn't complain, fortified by lazing about and by bathing in the brisk water, thick with salt, on which he would lie on his back and drift blissfully for hours.

Now they sit in a circle by the bonfire in the company of a Norwegian couple, an Englishman scraping out some tune on a guitar, a gloomy Swede, two laid-back German girls and a pimply Frenchman in dreadlocks. A one-and-a-half-litre bottle of grappa passes from hand to hand. Spicy Greek sausages stuck on sticks sizzle, on a wooden platter pieces of local Graviera cheese cut into strips crowd together, and English words and sentences flow from mouths propped up by waving hands.

Suddenly the pimply Frenchman lights a fat, long joint which, honoured by those present with one toke each, travels with due respect round the circle about the dying fire. This joint, with contents that have travelled a long way from Tunis in Tunisia to this island in the Cyclades, at some point lands in Jan's hands. And Jan, swaying with grappa and unaware that this is not ordinary tobacco, drags on it again and again and can't stop. The German girl, until now excited by the presence beside her of the handsome, though now cretinous-looking, Pole, suddenly snatches the joint from him like a predator and greedily sucks down what's left of it. A moment of awkward silence follows. Jan falls unconscious on the sand, and the others burst out laughing and the party starts all over again.

The next day, through long hours shot through with flashes of consciousness, the young Pole reaches the moment when he is able to crawl out of the hut and on all fours drag himself to the seashore, which flirts turquoise with the full moon ready to dive into its depths, and straining every muscle to the utmost, he stands up on his lower, wobbling limbs. Maybe it's only Poseidon accidentally snagging the hitherto sleeping surface of the sea with his trident, or maybe the unease, fear and despair welling up over the last few days make Jan let out a rending scream that turns into a howl, and then, when he slumps onto the sand again, this scream, now quieted, transforms into the words of a monologue.

– Mummy, help. I want to go home. You were right – don't trust that Greek. Where am I supposed to dig up the dough for the ship and the plane?

At this moment Sakis speaks up, having appeared on the beach a moment earlier.

– Oh, I see the unbearable lightness of being has crushed you.

– What are you talking about? And why are you cackling?

– What, haven't you read Kundera? She's a mistrustful one, that mum of yours, and as it happens I sold all my works today to the owner of a souvenir shop. Pack up your commie arse, get your stuff together, we're taking the night boat to Santorini.

It is still dark when the ship docks in the port.

What else awaits me here, thinks Jan, looking anxiously at the vertical wall of the caldera, up which, in a serpentine, winds a dimly lit road gouged out of the rock and the tephra. His anxiety grows when he stands packed like a herring in an overloaded bus, and the vehicle tilts dangerously towards the precipice on the left, or scrapes against the rocks on the right, and a young Greek, who earlier loaded the luggage onto the roof, unceremoniously shoves and squashes the passengers and sells them tickets for the ride.

When at last the bus reaches the flat ground at the top of the caldera, the tourists spontaneously clap, just as travellers often do after a plane lands. The sun emerging from the depths of the sea paints with light a magical panorama of the whole island. From this height you can see the breathtaking work of the volcano, which, erupting ages ago, tore apart the once round island and scattered its fragments, flooded them with lava that set hard and covered them with layers of pumice and ash. On the surviving surface now lie angular black, grey, brown and red stones, sprinkled over with fertile soil formed over the years, on which grow low, twisted vine bushes that look like green mushroom caps. In this way these plants are protected from the sun and the wind, and they retain moisture. Besides, their roots are forced to push deep into the soil to reach the groundwater.

The friends get off at a deserted stop. Before them winds a concrete road stretching among the vineyards, and in the distance you can see clusters of buildings climbing all the way to the top of a high, steep slope. They reach the edge of a sleepy, gloomy village and make their way along a narrow lane paved with slippery stones, on the sides of which doze the ruins of houses battered by earthquake and time.

On the left, by the shreds of a wall with fragments of plaster, a doorframe that has stayed upright bravely holds up a cracked wooden door, creaking at the slightest gust of wind. On the right, squeezed into the hollowed-out slope of pumice, a dwelling with the remains of windows and doors, and a little further on, and here's a surprise, a few buildings renovated with respect for tradition and two snow-white churches with blue domes glittering in the morning sun, adorned with climbing bougainvillea.

Although Jan, laden with his luggage and his worries, toils his way uphill, he can't ignore the fragrant feast to which the plants growing all around invite him. First the delicate scent of jasmine climbing one of the walls lures and charms him, then the sharp smell of wild oregano, which out of nowhere grows in clumps from a heap of rubble, lashes his nostrils, next a round, small-leaved basil, brushed by Sakis's hand, sends through its scent an unequivocal signal to the taste buds that a bit of breakfast would come in handy, and, as it were, for dessert Jan is treated to the sweet, cinnamon scent of carnations. They turn off the narrow lane and by steep stone steps reach a flat stretch of partly overgrown land.

– We're here – announces Sakis.

A fragrant Mediterranean pine, bent towards the path, greets them with a deep bow. Further on, behind a clump of reeds, grow pistachio trees with clusters of red-and-celadon, still unripe fruit. Further still, in a small vegetable garden, ripe miniature tomatoes smile, long peppers hidden among the leaves wink with flashes of reflected rays, aubergines charm with their gleaming violet, and finally yellow and green courgettes lying submissively on the moist soil, together with plump marrows, complete this vegetable menu. And at the very end, behind a low wall and a cracked terrace, laid with volcanic stones and poured over with concrete, stands a dwelling stuck to the pumice slope.

Sakis opens the door and invites Jan inside. The Pole breathes a sigh of relief – maybe it's not an apartment, but there are two mattresses with clean bedding, you can listen to music, in the kitchen there's running water, a fridge, a gas cooker, and on the terrace a makeshift shower. Never mind that plaster is falling off the walls, that the toilet is the surrounding bushes, a rounded shovel and a chair with a hole, but a hole big enough to fit an arse of any size.

And those magical evenings on the terrace over a bit of wine, mornings with Byzantine-style coffee, lounging on the beach under the tamarisks, bathing in brisk waters and bus trips around the island. And outings to the promenade of a nearby resort town, where his friend sells his works and afterwards they sit together in the Promyk bar. The owner of this bar is a close friend of Sakis's, Irek, a young local Greek. Jan likes to sit around here. He gets to know the taste of various drinks, listens to music unknown to him till now, meets people from Western Europe.

Tonight, though, it's different. Something is in the air. The owner of the bar neglects the regulars, even puts them off, playing Chinese vocal folk music through the speakers. The disgusted tourists, listening to the solo part of the singer wailing in a shrill voice, these customers hastily finish their drinks, pay, leave and look for some other bar, a normal one in their opinion. The pair of them, the owner Irek and Sakis, whisper something to each other conspiratorially and glance anxiously at a group of five young Greeks occupying the corner table. Uneasy, Jan wants to know what's going on, but only learns that these Greeks are probably plain-clothes policemen and that the owner urgently needs the help of the two friends.

The supposed policemen have already gone. Irek has closed the bar. Now the three of them walk through almost empty streets. Waiters clear the tables, turn off the lights, and only in one bar a band plays and sings live, and tipsy tourists are learning to dance the sirtaki. Irek leads them up an outside staircase to the first floor of a building surrounded by greenery and invites them into his flat. When they're inside, he opens the balcony door and then their nostrils are assaulted by the sharp, intense smell of Indian hemp growing in four plastic pots. Irek speaks to Sakis in Greek, and he translates for Jan into Polish.

– I think the cops have sniffed me out, we have to move these plants and hide them, but first I'll introduce them to you, look and admire, here are my three Graces: Sappho, Artemis and Siren, and this stunted one, though of the female sex, is called Papa-Manolis.

Very suggestive and vivid names, Jan concludes. Take Sappho, for instance, so dense, with tiny little leaves, plump, feminine and at the same time masculine, with taut, firm buds hungry for love, which weave poetic verses with their delicate little hairs. Then Artemis, with thick shoots and stems taut as bows, to which, ready to hunt down game, cling furry arrows. Then Siren, with long leaves flowing like fish in the song of the wind and the deceptively quivering little bells of her buds. And finally Papa-Manolis, short and stocky with a wavy beard, leafless, but instead with a cracked, massive seed cob resembling a climaxing vagina, endowed with an abundant, broad, thick moustache such as the brigands of Crete prided themselves on, whom for centuries the curved sabre of the Turk could not reach. At last they reached, in a Suzuki van, the edge of the village where Sakis's hut is, but not from the side Jan knew; this path leads to the longed-for goal and is moreover deserted and safe. The problem is how to transport this treasure, because it has to be done in one go; after all, old people still live here, they suffer from insomnia and wander about at night. And here Irek has a brilliant idea – he tears off the door, hanging from rusty hinges, from the ruins of a bakery that was famous years ago. On this door they place all four pots and set off. But at this moment something starts to happen: stones tumble with a crash from the rubble of the bakery, some unearthly light flares up, onto the path steps a figure, or rather the shadow of a figure – it is the owner of the bakery, old Andreas. He moves with difficulty on his ailing legs, but vigorously waves a long wooden oak paddle used for taking bread out of the oven. He tries to protest that they are stealing his door, but gives up, because he knows no one among the living will either hear him or see him. In the end, curious, he follows the three friends. Then the shadow of little Takis appears. During the occupation an SS man kicked this boy over a precipice. The child did not survive. And what for? For the little one stealing a piece of bread. Now Takis, as he used to do in the past, is lugging a bundle of dried thistles, which were once the payment for baking a loaf.

More Shadows join the caravan. The moustached guard Parys, from Crete, who from dawn to dusk patrolled the vineyards with a pistol in his holster, supplied with a three-litre wineskin of sweet wine and oat rusks hard as stones. The widow Sofia with a tin bucket, ready to draw water from the rainwater cistern to water the basil adorning the entrances to several local churches. And finally Babis, who crashed, hitting the trunk of a eucalyptus, while riding his motorbike to the bank after a sleepless night. The caravan reaches Sakis's house. The Shadows settle down on the low wall and watch the friends, who hide the three Graces by the wall of an unused stable behind a clump of wild fig and camouflage Papa-Manolis in a separate, safe place. Then they sit down at the table on the terrace and, sipping grappa on ice, rest. From time to time the shadows maliciously disturb their blissful peace – now a pile of stones slides down with a crash, a sudden gust of wind noisily tugs at the dried reeds, a frightened dog howls, a startled owl hoots and loudly flaps its wings. And now Irek, after a moment's absence, appears carrying a fat XXL-size joint and announces:

– Here we have two pinches from Papa-Manolis's beard. We'll smoke it, and then we'll go to the church to bow before the icon of the Mother of God.

Jan, sceptical about the idea at first, in the end lets himself be talked into it.

An hour passes and the caravan sets off again. Jan is calm, because the weed didn't work on him, while the two Greeks are clearly disappointed, though they comment with humour that Papa-Manolis promised, twitched his whiskers, flirted with his grey beard, invited them on a journey to other dimensions, and turned out to be plain hay. The unusual pilgrims leave the village and head towards a hill drowning in lamplight, on whose summit, nestled against slender cypresses, stands a solitary church. The Shadows recognise the narrow road, chipped by the passage of time, paved with volcanic stones. Some of the vineyard terraces seem familiar, and a few neglected fig trees bearing figs too small to interest people, but a blessing for the birds that rest here on their journey to Africa. When the caravan reaches a small cemetery, a shiver of horror runs through Jan, because his senses unexpectedly feel the physical presence of the Shadows. The other two young men feel the same, but their reaction is quite different. They stare at each other, nod their heads approvingly and raise their hands high. Papa-Manolis has surprised them, they haven't had a kick like that in years. The sly old moustache. And they are all seized with uncontrollable laughter, even the Shadows stop brooding over the beautiful old days of their lives and giggle, amused. The night is drawing to an end, soon the fifteenth of August will dawn, the most important day of Greek Orthodoxy, when liturgies are held in honour of the Mother of God, who appears in this country under many names. The caravan has already reached the church of the Panagia Episkopi, where there is a miraculous icon, fished out of the sea years ago by local fishermen. The previous evening a service was celebrated, after which the faithful shared split-pea hummus, black beans and sweet altar wine. Now the night vigil is going on, but every newcomer can bow before the miraculous icon and then partake of the gifts of this patch of earth. Jan, to whom the Orthodox rite is unknown, follows Irek step by step. First they light wax candles for their loved ones. Then Irek stops at one icon of a saint after another, making the sign of the cross differently from a Polish Catholic, until he reaches the famous image, sits down on a bench and gives himself over to prayer. On the stone floor, old women in black sit astride, humming Byzantine psalms. One of them, worn out by the vigil, slumps down and falls asleep. The Shadows occupy the inside of the whole church, winding themselves around the columns, clinging to the frescoes, nestling against the icons, flickering in the glow of the candles. Their words of prayer, whispered to the rhythm of the dancing flames, join with the voices of the women and the church fills with a song that spreads, flows out into the courtyard, merges with the rustle of the cypresses and turns into a polyphony of the senses, not only of sounds but also of colours, smells, tastes and touches. Take the flutter of sparrows' wings, and maybe of angels' too, the monotonous yapping of a dog, the tender twittering of a mother and an infant, or the grunting of the wind romping among the dried thistles. Or the violet of oregano revived by the morning dew, the pink, orange, yellow and pale-cream streaks of sunlight reflected on the surface of the sea, or the white of shy clouds quickly fading in the blue sky. The smells of round clumps of basil tousled by hands, the resinous scent of cypress cones, or the appetising aroma of lamb roasted on the grill, marinated in thyme and garlic, which the faithful will be treated to after the morning liturgy. The bitterish, salty-sweet and herbal taste of olive oil, marjoram and browned onion in the boiled black beans, or the honey-and-raisin taste of the sweet wine. And finally of the touch of lips in kisses of greeting and farewell, of handshakes while giving good wishes, of dry grasses rubbing against the stone wall around the church, or of the caresses of brisk breezes with which the wind bestows the sweaty bodies. Filled with a symphony of sensations, Jan and the two young Greeks sit on the low stone wall. They treat themselves to wheat bread, hummus of split peas and tahini, and black beans. They sample the sweet home-made wine, and the Shadows crowd around them, sorry that they cannot take part in this modest meal. The sun climbs imperceptibly up the August sky, and the Shadows fade, go out and turn into indefinite reflections from another dimension of space-time. They probably won't return to the ruined village any more. Maybe they have found peace? They no longer have to patrol the vineyards, water the plants, guard the bakery, haul bundles of dry thistle… Could it be that the caravan has reached its goal, the oasis, Jan wonders as he comes down the hill with his companions. But no, I'm going home soon, Irek has his life here, and Sakis doesn't yet know where he's headed, the young Pole adds in his thoughts.

Jan rocks in time to the music, sitting in a rocking chair. He has put on a Vangelis record called “Ocean”. On the calendar hanging on the wall the date shows – January of the year two thousand twenty-one. Outside the window, over the busy street, fine snow is falling. Along the opposite pavement passers-by in masks hurry along. On the outside windowsill three sparrows hop about comically, fly off for a moment, then come back and with noisy chirping and the flutter of little wings fight over grains of hulled sunflower seeds. It seems that a storm is raging on the Greek composer's ocean. A lazy tear runs down the wrinkled face of the tired man, who massages his aching neck, rubs ointment into his stiff knees, then presses his hand to the place under his ribs where his liver is, gets up from the chair with difficulty and, leaning on his cane, shuffles towards the bathroom, letting off a loud fart on the way. During his absence from the room the music of Vangelis seems to wander like a camera's eye over the shelves on which photographs are set out, documenting the years that have passed since that memorable holiday in Greece. After a while he comes back with a feeling of relief. Again he is forced to get up when the intercom buzzer sounds. He opens the door and takes a small rectangular parcel from the courier. When he realises who the sender is, he smiles, moved, sits down in the chair briskly this time and unwraps the little package. On his knees rests a book, a collection of poems by the Greek Nobel laureate Giorgos Seferis. He leafs through the pages, reads the table of contents, goes back to the beginning of the little volume and in the note “From the Author” notices a sentence underlined in pencil:

“Poetry is like air; if poetry runs out, people will stop breathing.”

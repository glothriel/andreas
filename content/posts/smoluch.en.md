Through the streets of a city of a hundred thousand in western Poland an ambulance races with its siren on. In the ambulance lies eight-year-old Tomek, hooked up to a respirator. The boy’s eyelids are assaulted by flashes of red light, and scraps of conversation between the driver and the paramedic break through the howl of the siren to reach his ears.

– What kind of people are these, to bring a little kid to such a state?
– Well what? You saw for yourself what was going on in there.
– It’s nicotine poisoning for sure.
– Don’t give me that, look who’s turned doctor.
– For your information, I usually know what’s what.

The half-conscious boy remembers – he is sitting on the bed, bored, they told him to sleep, he looks at the photo of his father, who is somewhere in Germany and supposedly works there, and in the same photo his mother gazing at her husband as if at a holy picture, his dear mum, who is now standing at the conveyor belt on the night shift in a tobacco factory producing cigarettes of a well-known brand.

They told him to sleep, but behind the door he hears his sister, who has invited guests, and a rowdy party is in full swing out there. Tomek throws his phone onto the bed. He sneaks into the room where his sister’s guests are partying and settles down in a corner. The whole space of the room is filled with clouds of smoke, Tomek coughs, chokes, searches for a sip of fresh air, then he comes across a bottle of some drink and mechanically and greedily drinks the contents, which are sweet and delicious, on the label he makes out the words – grape, sweet “SAMOS”, and dazed he slumps down in the corner.

Now smoke rings dance under the ceiling, a white dragon leaps off the table towards him, and grey monkeys romp on the heads of his sister and her mates, to then dissolve into wisps and hide in the nooks of the room.

The boy feels that he is floating in the air and from up high he sees his body hooked up to some apparatus. The same thing is happening to the men lying on the two beds next to him. After a moment the three of them drop to the floor of the hospital ward, and then run in a panic to their bodies. They hear the doctor’s orders, the nurses, busy carrying out those orders, pay them no attention, and they vainly try to make contact, to touch anything at all, everything around is only pictures made of air.

Behind the glass his sister sits on a chair, wrapped in her own arms, sobbing, tears drip sparingly onto her fashionable jeans marked with fancy slashes, and out of the corner of her eye she follows the big posters – on the first a baby has a cigarette in its mouth instead of a dummy, on the others you can see rotten, decomposing fingers, gums and teeth, pained, pre-agonal faces hooked up to respirators, and on each one slogans along the lines of – smoking kills, causes lung cancer, wipes out potency etc.

Next to her stands the mother with her pale face stuck to the glass, which is streaming with tears and which she pushes against as if she wanted to break through it and hug her son’s beloved body. Tomek runs up to his mother and sister with resurrected hope, but meets only air. His sister doesn’t react at all, and his mother, shaken, peels herself away from the glass and casts a lost gaze around.

Suddenly a guard appears in the hospital corridor in a black uniform, his face and hands blackened with something dark brown, he looks disapprovingly at Tomek, and then calls the two men over and orders all three of them to follow him. Evidently the hospital ward is connected to the cigarette factory, because the procession of shadows, passing through a discreet door, ends up in a huge hall. You can smell the intoxicating smell of tobacco and hear the steady noise of the belt rolling along thousands of cigarettes.

They pass heaps of unprocessed tobacco leaves and through a recess in the wall they reach some stairs, which they take down to the basement part of the building. There, winding through the half-dark, they reach a poorly lit room, where an individual in black pounces on them and expertly sprays them with something dark brown and sticky.

Tomek discovers that this something dries quickly and becomes one with the skin of the face and hands, he also discovers that in the room women and men sit on chairs, treated the same way as they were.

There is, however, someone in this gathering who is different from the others – it is an elegant middle-aged gentleman with clean hands and a tanned face, who is addressing those present, but at the moment when he notices the newcomers he breaks off for a moment and then adds:

– Since new guests have joined our gathering, I will repeat it once more. Well then, if any of you wants to get well and return to the living, he has to win ten new customers who smoke cigarettes of our brand. Only then will his treatment be generously subsidised by the tobacco consortium which I represent. – And pointing at the boy he added: – This little Tarball, who dragged him in here? He is too young to act on his own, so I am assigning the boy a guardian. Mr Trawka, you will give the little one a few of your precious hours.

Along a crowded street two tarry shadows drift, passing people in masks.

– You see for yourself, little one, in these conditions it’s not easy to win over a lover of smoking, let alone of smoking a particular brand of cigarettes. I’ll let you in on a few secrets, but after that you’ve got to manage on your own.

Trawka leads Tomek up to a kiosk, cigarettes of various brands are on display in the window, and Trawka lays his hand on the glass and smears the tarry substance so that only the pack from his own home factory stays in view. A pimply teenager stops in front of the kiosk and, after a moment’s thought, buys cigarettes of that particular brand, the Brutus brand.

Then the two of them stop in front of a café. A young couple, sitting at a table, are sipping their drinks, poking straws into their mouths, he pulls a pack of “Brutuses” out of his pocket and offers the woman one, she refuses and reaches for a pack of long, thin cigarettes, and here Trawka steps in – he puffs out his lips, takes in air, waves his sooty paws, blows with a vengeance, and the pack flies off and lands in a puddle of dirty water.

A moment later the woman, as if nothing had happened, lights up a Brutus. Trawka winks at Tomek, from his left hand a tarry, stinking goo runs off, dripping, and the hand takes on the colour of natural skin.

– You see, little one, but don’t be fooled, it’s only half a success, there’s no knowing if these people will definitely go on preferring our brand.

The two sooty figures, winding through the streets, reach the back of a secondary school building and come upon a group of teenagers who, gesticulating, shouting over each other and shoving, are smoking cigarettes.

– I’ve shown you a few tricks, I’ve pointed out a few strategic locations, now I’m leaving you, you’ve got to manage on your own.

Tears run down the boy’s face as he watches Trawka, dragging his feet and with a joint in his hand, move away and disappear behind the school building. Sitting on a bench by the school pitch, he watches the teenagers – one runs up to a stray ball, kicks it towards the girls who are playing around, and comes back out of breath, another coughs and spits out thick phlegm, another blows fancy little rings out of his mouth, and yet another, a boy who is clearly overweight, stands a few steps away and stares ahead miserably, laughed at and not let into the company by the others.

Tomek wipes away his tears, he remembers the clouds of choking smoke, the hospital ward, the sooty, resigned figures, his mother and sister. He springs up from the bench. With a gleam in his eyes he takes in air. He runs up to the teenagers and blows.

Fat drops of rain fall from the sky, it thunders and flashes, a violent wind snatches the boys’ packs of cigarettes, which whirl around and scatter their contents in all directions, and the butts stick to their hands and burn painfully. Tomek leaves the place with gusto. Running, he passes the café gardens. The customers hide indoors, and soaked packs of cigarettes lie strewn about in the puddles.

The boy wakes up in the hospital ward. He raises the upper part of his body and props himself on his elbows. He frees himself from the respirator, jumps out of bed and runs into his mother’s arms.

In an autumn park, on a carpet of fallen pastel-yellow ginkgo leaves, by the playground, Tomek’s sister sits watching her brother climbing the ladder. Her girlfriends walk past. They are smoking cigarettes and, noticing her, they giggle ironically. And she raises her hand and shows them her middle finger.

The end.

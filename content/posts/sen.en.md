The nineteen-sixties, a town in western Poland. Ten-year-old Paweł is still asleep, restlessly moving his arms and legs and pushing aside the eiderdown, which slides off onto the floor. His mother lights a wax candle by the little picture of the Mother of God in its gilded frame and wakes him, asking him to wash and come to breakfast, while his father lights the fire in the tiled stove. The sleepy boy turns over onto his other side, mumbles something under his breath and falls asleep again. The parents do not wake their son and leave the house, having left on the kitchen table a breakfast consisting of milk in a glass bottle, a wheat roll with a piece of curd cheese and a slice of bread spread with butter and sprinkled with sugar. On the table there is also a note that says: don't be late for school, don't forget your breakfast, kisses. Paweł is still asleep, his body lies on the metal bed, but he himself feels light as a feather from a bird's wing and he rises, bounces, touches the ceiling, lands on the windowsill and jumps. He flutters, he flies over the street. Beneath him, along the cobbled roadway, an open army truck drives past. In the back sit young men with eastern features, who bounce up and down, clinging to the wooden benches, and straighten their caps with stars with the hammer and sickle. The truck stops in front of a guardhouse by a three-metre concrete fence. The boy drops lower, almost touching the sentries, lands on the other side of the fence and runs joyfully, bounding in ten-metre stride-flights. He peers into the shop for Soviet officers in this villa district closed to Poles and greedily devours with his eyes the chocolates, the biscuits, the little jars of figs in syrup and other delicacies never seen in Polish shops, then with a decisive leap he takes off and is once again above his own street. He soars high over the roofs of the tenement houses, which are grey, wounded by bullets and with holes in their roofs. Paweł pulls his knees up to his chin and lands softly in a large yard in front of a wall which is probably what is left of a German stable, because stoneware, brick-built mangers are set against that wall. Beside it stands a massive horse chewing oats and straw, and a vicious mongrel runs about at a safe distance, yapping at the great, patient animal. Now the boy looks into a pen where hens run about clucking noisily, pecking at grain, at tiny pebbles, squabbling, attacking some chosen wretch, and the rooster breaks them up with decisive blows of his beak. In the second pen three young hogs jostle at the trough and fight for the best access to the slops thickened with dry bread. In the last one, shut in hutches, mournful rabbits with drooping ears twitch their whiskers in resignation. Paweł jumps up high, flies off again over the tar-papered roof and lands right by the bakery, in front of which boys are wolfing down steaming rolls and a six-year-old girl lugs a huge loaf and diligently picks at the heel of the bread. The boys are making plans for games after school – maybe we'll play pilnik, or maybe gear-wheel races after all, or a rag-ball match against the next courtyard, and why not penknife, or with the girls knock-her-off-the-horse or “kucha”, or maybe hide-and-seek, or buttons? Then they plot how they might give the Russkies a kicking, and the chance comes along – with the measured clatter of iron-shod boots a column of Soviet soldiers approaches, and the boys leap out onto the pavement and shout – Wania sapagi waniajut!

Then they scarper, hiding in the gateways. Paweł envies the boys a little, but he prefers flying anyway. Gliding low over the street, he passes the old Jew's tailor's shop and the crippled Greek's shoemaker's workshop and lands in front of the grocer's. Inside stand barrels of pickled cucumbers and sauerkraut and salted herrings, crates of orangeade, milk in litre bottles, sour cream, beer, fruit wine and soda water. Women with string bags and cloth shopping bags stand in the queue and gossip, and the shop lady cuts slices of marmalade and butter from angular blocks, wraps them in brown paper, lays them on the tall scales, reads off the decagrams and grams and works it out in złoty, scribbling with a pencil in a squared exercise book. An impatient, hungover type with several days' stubble swaps empty barrel-shaped beer bottles for full ones, and Paweł greedily scans the glass-fronted shelves where sweets of every kind are piled up: toffees, iris toffees, krówki fudges, fruit drops and murzynki, into which an aluminium scoop is stuck, promising that it will dive at any moment into all the compartments, in order to then fill a paper bag rolled into a cone with colourful goodies. The boy reluctantly leaves the shop window and rises above the neighbouring, darkened street, along which a line of vehicles is passing – at the front a Gazik with soldiers at combat readiness glides over the cobbles, then a black Volga, then in turn a stately limousine, also black, a Chaika, again a black Volga and finally a Gazik armed to the teeth. The few passers-by stare at this cavalcade with their gobs wide open, and a gent with a walking stick and a little hat with a peacock feather whispers under his breath – fucking Soviet lordlings. Paweł rises in gentle flight and hovers over the tram stop. Among those waiting the boy notices his parents – his father lights a “Giewont”, and his mother runs her gaze over the sky, blinking, as if she senses something, some whitened shadow, the rustle of falling leaves, or maybe of wings, she feels her pulse quicken and looks questioningly at her husband, who calms her with a hug, and the cigarette smoke spreads above them the blurred shape of a figure hanging in the air. With a noisy screech of braking metal wheels on the rails, spraying thousands of sparks above their heads, the tram stops at the stop. Paweł's mother turns her head once more, looks around uneasily, and then together with her husband and the other passengers she gets on and they ride away. Several carts roll across the street, harnessed to exhausted horses with thick fetlocks. The wagons are loaded with jute sacks of potatoes, crates of greens, vegetables and fruit, and even live hens, which protest loudly, frightened. One of the horses stops and pisses, leaving a sprawling puddle behind it, and another, tail raised, scatters little heaps of dung over the cobbles. The boy floats through the air along the tram route, watching from above the sparks spraying over the electric wires, and after a while lands at the stop from which his parents walk off and vanish into the crowd. He now finds himself in front of the great building of a pre-war theatre, into which, through wooden gates reinforced with iron fittings, an army truck drives, the rest of the entrances and windows are boarded up. With one long bound Paweł leaves the market square, glides in low flight over a dead street where ruined, low tenements loom, and lands in the school yard. A crowd of girl and boy pupils in uniforms, with badges on their sleeves and cardboard satchels, throngs at the entrance to the building to the accompaniment of the bell announcing the start of lessons. Paweł comes back from school with a light step, barely touching the pavement, and stops at the stop on the market square, where a crowd of people is thronging. The oncoming tram does not stop at once, first it ploughs into the crowd, then the driver brakes desperately and the vehicle jumps the rails, smashing the display windows of a bookshop, from which, from the shop window, spill the collected works of Lenin, volumes in hard leather bindings and with gold lettering on a red ground. The boy sits shocked on the cobbles and tries to get up, but his body refuses to obey and he only watches, helpless, as people heave and jostle, some lie motionless, others groan, still others scream, wring their hands or raise their arms to the sky. A group of Soviet military men pushes through the crowd, lifts a man's body, lays it on a stretcher and moves off towards the theatre building, and out of the corner of his eye Paweł notices that it is his father. With difficulty he gets up, wants to run, but cannot, then ambulances appear at the scene of the accident and into one of them they carry his mother, and the boy tries to jump, to fly after the vehicle, but in vain, and the ambulance drives off. With difficulty, step by step, he drags himself towards where they carried his father. The sentries do not notice the shadow that slips into the inside of the theatre. In a spacious room crates of grenades and rifles are piled up, Paweł passes them and seeps through the wall into the auditorium, where on heaps of rubble lie strewn smashed seats, portraits of actors in shattered frames with German captions, and a tangled, black, dusty curtain. He climbs a steep staircase and realises that behind his back there is nothing any more, because, wanting to turn back, he falls into the void and at the last moment grabs hold of the stairs, pulls himself up and climbs upwards again.

Now he is in a vast attic, where the roof, suddenly burst apart by a mighty shock, scatters into a thousand pieces of roof tile. He wants to turn back but there is nowhere to go, so he runs, balancing on the lattice of horizontal beams, loses his balance, falls into an abysmal darkness and lands in a glade overgrown with lush green. The grass is rough, it clings with a relaxing dampness to his feet and winds upwards, reaching his knees, and then greedily tries to pull Paweł down into its roots. The boy frees himself from this trap, sits down on a flat rock and looks around – at the edge of the glade stretches a dense, unnatural forest made up of badly trimmed trees, as if moved here from an English park. Crowded heaps of cubist blocks in oppressive pastel colours and with black trunks and boughs which, rubbing against one another, scatter a choking dust all around, these heaps press in on Paweł and crush him with paralysing fear. Out of the forest emerges a group of Soviet officers in dress uniforms, led by the sinister silhouette of Stalin, who surveys the surroundings with a contemptuous, cold gaze and, gesticulating, gives orders, and the boy, driven by the desire to free himself from this gloomy landscape, leaps up from the rock, runs straight ahead, until he reaches the steep stairs again. He no longer knows whether he wants to go back or rather to flee, to climb somewhere high, somewhere his pounding heart is driving him. Up these stairs, struggling, now on all fours, now on trembling legs, he reaches the attic and once again moves along the beams, slowly at first, then faster and faster, because everything he passes crumbles behind his back and falls into the dark abyss. Finally he realises that around him there is nothing left but a single beam, on which he sits astride and floats, sways, sinks, rises like a lonely boat on a boundless ocean. But it is not an ocean at all, it is an empty, silent space, which thickens with the depth of the gaze, giving the impression that the boy feels as if he were at the centre of a sphere wrapped in a horizon whose whiteness the eye cannot pierce. From this horizon a small cloud breaks away and, coming closer to him, takes on the colour of whitened cadmium yellow and clings to his body, bestows a soothing moisture, seeps in, dissolves and neutralises every fear, all his worries, and he remembers, remembers that he can fly. He takes the first step, then a second, for a moment he hangs in the void, then rises higher and higher, in gentle, slow flight, and the beam, turning on its own axis, drops out of the inside of the spherical horizon and vanishes. Flying, he feels that the cloud flows off him, breaks down into particles of marble dust and arranges itself into a musical stave, on which notes dance, pulsing, and he hears music materialising in the form of silk threads. These threads wind around him vertically, horizontally, slantwise to the right and to the left, forming a cocoon, which suddenly flies off, breaks through the milky horizon, stops in the void, where music sounds with rising intensity, until the moment when the cocoon bursts and the threads unravel, only to braid together again a moment later and weave around Paweł, out of phrases of an adagio, a flying vehicle like a saucer racing at the speed of light. The boy sits dumbstruck in the seat, watches the vehicle's instruments and monitors and makes out a misty figure formed of glowing particles of marble, and then, starting from his feet, capillaries appear on his body filled with the venom of fear, which gives birth to contradictory, mutually warring thoughts and questions – to believe, not to believe, to search or to go back, white or black, good or evil… All this makes Paweł grow heavy, he yields to the law of gravity, his feet break through the floor of the vehicle, he falls slowly like an autumn leaf and lands on a rocky ledge of a mountain ridge. His first impulse is to retreat along the path winding among angular boulders towards the gloomy space below, but he cannot, because the boulders slide away together with an avalanche of stones and clods of earth and all of it is swallowed by the greedy abyss. So he toils uphill towards the summit, and after every step the path behind his back crumbles and vanishes into the dead emptiness. He reaches a flat ledge, which is the end of the path, and realises that apart from this one support in the form of a cracked rock column there is absolutely nothing left, only the inside of an empty sphere surrounded on all sides by a horizon of thickened space. Then the fear and all his worries flow out of him, thicken, clump together into a single mass and, as a slippery stone, fall and vanish. And he becomes light and darts through the air, or rather rises higher and higher, reaching a horizon from another dimension, which splits into layers, forming clouds of marble dust, and these in turn unfurl into braids of silk thread, threads arranging themselves into thousands of staves and crumbled into millions of notes. An omnipresent, unceasing music sounds, which seems to hold the whole world in balance and protects the universe from total disintegration.

A small bundle of thread-like notes wraps the boy in a transparent bubble and carries him, lifts him on a song of knowing towards the horizon, which keeps receding, until at last with the final chord of this song the bubble bursts, and he lands on a fluffy surface made of transparent particles of marble, where in the glaze of colours warm hues dominate, only slightly clouded with zinc white. The boy, his eyes lowered, carefully places his steps on this incoherent, it would seem, land, but his feet do not sink, they even rise, absorbing the omnipresent music. He closes his eyes, whirls in a dervish dance, turning now to the left, now to the right, with outstretched arms he dives forwards and backwards, and then lands on soft down pulsing with the sounds of Chopin's sonatas, opens his eyes and makes out oval rock masses emerging, made of the same thickened matter. With a sense unknown to him until now he feels figures moving behind his back, but as soon as he turns his head, the figures crumble into dust, and this dust in turn fuses, sticks together, forming elaborate tools, which press against the rock masses and sculpt, gouging, cutting and removing the superfluous chips of stone, until from one of the rocks there emerges a fragment of a wing and the profile of a face resembling the face of his father. With the same newly acquired sense he reads the meaning of the whispers recited in singing voices.

– This little being is filled with love.

– Yes, but it is not yet its time.

– Perhaps one day it will come back to us.

Slowly he is paralysed by a longing that creeps stealthily over him, he grows heavy, his feet sink into the fluffy matter of the surface, he drowns in it first up to his knees, then right up to his neck, out of the corner of his eye he still sees the angular rock masses crumble to dust, and out of this dust arise wavering silk threads, dancing and throbbing with music that grows ever quieter for him. He closes his eyes and with sorrow gives himself up to falling. At last he lands on damp, soft soil, hugging, from the momentum, the rough trunk of a tree. He opens his eyes and, moving away from the trunk, takes in a forest sunk in half-darkness, made up of tall trees with bare trunks, crowned at their tops with spreading canopies. He notices a path winding among shrubby plants and sets off slowly ahead, but the dense wall of the undergrowth stops him, surrounds him, creeping with roots visible above the soil, and he stands there disoriented and watches the world around him, unknown to him. He hears the rustle of fragments of withered plants rubbing against one another, the symphony of dewdrops breaking into thousands of particles, the echo, the memory of the music from the previous dimension, and tries in vain to recall the words whispered behind his back by indeterminate beings, whitened shadows, entities filling the void with the sounds of billions of strings. In vain. He looks closely at a bush reaching up to his knees, with juicy, jagged leaves armed with thorns, of a colour made of all the shades of warm green, a bush from whose centre three slender stems climb, swaying, from which white bubbles grow, bursting at the same moment, and out of them fly winged pollen grains curling into two big wings that rise up and vanish above the treetops. These stems are crowned with three flowers, and each flower is different. The first, of indeterminate colour, with a source of tangled, sparkling rays enclosed in a transparent membrane, from which these rays flow out in fountains of light piercing the gloom of the forest floor. The second in the shape of a transparent chalice, delicately tinted with a thinned, burgundy red, and in the middle of this chalice two pistils crossed with each other, and at the place of this joining a white drop, constantly spreading and constantly setting, from which a blood-red liquid seeps. The third flower is a snow-white sphere of diamond down, which sprays everywhere and never runs out. These three stems intertwine and out of this trinity a new flower is born, with petals as big as sails, which curl slightly and catch the boy, bedding him down in stamens intoxicatingly fragrant with nectar. The plant grows, winds, climbs higher and higher, until it breaks through the treetops, and there the flower opens its petals and releases Paweł from its embrace, and he freezes for a moment in the void, and then spreads his arms and glides in descending flight and realises that he has lost the ability to rise, that he can only fall gently now. Flying like this, he ends up in a dark void, which swallows him, until finally he lands in the attic of the theatre. Making his way through the half-darkness, he goes down the steep stairs and reaches a sufficiently lit corridor.

He is surprised by the drastic changes – there are no traces of destruction anywhere, the wooden floor gleaming with varnish is laid with a red runner, on the smooth, white walls hang photographs of Polish actors and photographs with shots of scenes from theatre productions. Through the half-open oak door he looks into the auditorium, where seats gleaming with cleanliness stand in rows, and the stage is hidden by a new dark-red curtain. Up in the gods a worker in a green uniform is setting up the spotlights, and Paweł goes down the wide marble stairs and comes out in front of the theatre building, which spreads itself out majestically with freshly painted plaster, new doors and windows and an ornamental façade. By the entrance, in display cases, hang advertising posters and sheets with the printed repertoire, and right next to them, on the paving of granite setts, stand little tables and parasols. He notices that his right hand is resting on a wooden walking stick and that this hand is wrinkled and trembles slightly, and in the reflection on the glass of the display case he sees an old, grey-haired man with a tired face. He sits down, dazed, on a little bench and takes in the world around him. A young man walks past, he has some black object stuck in his ear, from which hangs a wire disappearing into his pocket, and this man is talking to himself. Nearby two girls stand talking. One has a spiky dark-blue mop of hair on her head, the other is covered in tattoos and a metal ring hangs from her nose. A bloke with a shaved head sits down on the bench, takes a strange rectangular object out of his bag, opens it as if he were opening a book, taps the keys with his fingers, and pictures, letters and numbers appear on the screen. All around there are advertisements for unknown products with inscriptions in an unknown language. Nowhere are there any tram rails to be seen, and the familiar tenements have been replaced by buildings that resemble constructions made of cardboard boxes. A young couple approaches Paweł. He tall, with a goatee and an original haircut, she with her head crowned by a mane of Titian, fiery-red hair. The young man, gesticulating, clearly worried, turns to Paweł.

– Dad, where have you been wandering off to again? Mum's going out of her mind. Get up, we're going home.

The three of them walk away, mingling with a crowd of people in masks, people who look at each other suspiciously and pass each other in wide arcs. Ten-year-old Paweł feels something damp and silky on his face and opens his eyes. A hard-working tongue sticks out of a shaggy muzzle, a little higher gentle eyes shine, and the edge of the frame is filled with a joyfully dancing tail. Paweł hugs the dog, pats it on the neck and glances at the standing clock. He jumps out of bed and gets dressed in a hurry…

THE END

(I dedicate this story to my son and my wife)

Notes. Kucha – a game, a children's pastime. Wańka, sapagi waniajut – Wańka (from Ivan), your galoshes stink. Volga – a make of Soviet car.

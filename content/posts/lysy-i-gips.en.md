Into a small, shabby little shop with building materials walks a bald guy in his middle years and looks around at the shelves and the floor. I'm a sculptor – he says to the shop assistant – and I need plaster for a mould to cast a small figurine of Venus. And the assistant replies – by all means, I have a domestic product here, inexpensive, and points to a package lying on the shelf.

That crap I'm definitely not buying – Baldy almost shouts, and the clerk says – easy now, my dear sir, I'll bring you something high-class in a moment, something foreign. At that moment, inside the package, a small lump of petrified plaster shook the dust off itself and yelled to itself – what a prick, he'll remember me yet. And on the word GIPS the only embossed letter, namely the letter P, began to swell with rage and spill over the whole label. And the sculptor adds “just look at that, what a sloppy sticker” and walks out of the shop with the imported plaster. He stands at the top of the steps and imagines a sumptuous, gilded Venus dripping with glints of desire, opens his eyes and greedily follows buttocks swaying like boats in tight shorts, takes a step to go down to the pavement and suddenly the world turns upside down on him. He lies on the cobbles howling with pain. Out of the corner of his eye he notices firm breasts heaving on the boat and howls even louder. The curious assistant from the building materials shop witnesses a scene in which a crowd of onlookers parts, a paramedic gets out of an ambulance, examines the injured man and announces – fractured shinbone, plaster needed, the clinic has run out. The assistant offers a free package of the domestic plaster, and the lump practically jumps for joy inside, whispering – got you now, baldy. The artist lies on his bed, dejected, his leg imprisoned from ankle to knee in plaster, on which various inscriptions, dedications, little jokes are scattered, and on the part by the foot there is a realistically painted picture of an ironic mug sticking out its tongue. Beside the bed colourful, fragrant flowers delight the senses. From the next room comes the voice of a woman, who promises that supper, a drop of wine, etc., but first a surprise. A moment later she appears undressed and lies down beside the dumbstruck sculptor, kisses deeply, encourages his manhood with her hand, mounts him and the couple give themselves over to ardent frolics. At the critical moment that petrified lump of plaster crumbles, the artist's leg gets jammed and he can't pull away, and the woman, instead of helping the two bodies come apart, clings to him even harder and the shot hits the bull's-eye. A chain of events follows – not suited to each other, constantly quarrelling, they divorce, and she announces that she has landed an engagement, and goes off to her mum at the other end of Poland. Then a little daughter comes into the world. After a few years, during which he often visits them and regularly sends money, the artist begins to doubt his paternity and decides to undergo a test. And what about the plaster? Well, Baldy had at one time decided to keep the piece with the painted mug and put that bit of plaster on a shelf as a warning to himself. The sculptor knocks on the door of the surgery; it is opened by an obese lady in her middle years in an apron that was once white, in a headband stretched tight over curly blond hair, and she says.

“To establish whether you're a daddy or a sucker, we'll need a sample.” The woman, her buttocks smacking at every step, pushing forward her ample bosom, raises her hand, curled into a spoon, palm up, to the height of the artist's waist, moves her moist tongue, winks her right eyelid and adds.

“Need a hand, sweetie?” Baldy blushes, excuses himself, saying he feels embarrassed, and hurriedly hides behind the screen, and there he comes across a pile of porn magazines, which he contemptuously avoids with his eyes, and he closes his eyes, imagining a small, gilded sculpture of Venus. The figurine grows, takes on the dimensions and shapes of a grown woman, and the gold layer flows in a pulsing stream between the artist's legs, uncovering naked cobwebs of pleasure. The sculptor becomes the victim of his own work and a moment later the little container is full of genetic material for testing. Some time later the artist learns that the result is negative, and in his first impulse, looking at the shelf where, from the bit of plaster, the malicious mug winks its little eyes and shows its slippery tongue, he means to stamp on the exhibit and throw it in the rubbish, but in the end he gives up on it and says to himself.

“To hell with all that money spent, those sleepless nights on trains, the hours in station bars, the womanish accusations and grievances, to hell with it, let the little girl grow up healthy and let the mother find a daddy for her…” Now the neighbours see Baldy striding upright along the pavement in a black hat, a long coat and with a canvas bag that says “buy only Polish products”. And the mug on the bit of plaster has almost faded, and only the left eye sometimes gleams and maliciously winks.
